STORY
Every player is an alien visitor studying life on Earth and filing a report
to the home world. Each world answers one question for that report (World 1:
what is alive here and how is it built?). Each level opens with a one-line
"mission log" and ends with "report sent". The final Keystone Challenge is
the full report. Students design their own alien on the start screen: body
shape, color, number of eyes, antennae. Gear (goggles, cape, hats tied to
the biology content) unlocks with stars. Looks never change abilities, and
the design is saved on the student's device.

GAME DESIGN
- Genre: side-scrolling platformer with 3D depth ("2.5D"). At least four
  background layers scrolling at different speeds for parallax, plus lighting
  and depth effects.
- Controls: arrow keys or WASD to move, spacebar to jump, one action key.
  Also on-screen touch buttons for tablets and phones.
- The first level teaches the controls by playing. No text tutorial screens.
- Biology must be part of the play, not a quiz pasted between levels.
  Examples: collect monomers to build a polymer bridge; choose the correct
  transport type to pass through a membrane gate; sort organelles; match
  codons; a boss battle answered with exam-style questions.
- Levels last 2 to 4 minutes. Wrong answers cost a little time or health and
  show a one-sentence explanation, then let the student try again.
- Missed terms come back more often in later levels.

LEARNING DESIGN (research-based)
Every game mechanic must teach the biology itself, not decorate a quiz.
1. Many ways to answer, not only multiple choice. Rotate these activities:
   - Become It: the player turns into a biological agent and must follow
     its rules (a water molecule in osmosis, an mRNA courier, a hormone in
     a feedback loop, energy moving up a food chain).
   - Build It: collect parts in the correct order to assemble something
     (a protein from codons, a polymer from monomers, organelle to organism).
   - Specimen Sort: drag cards into labeled bins (a digital card sort).
   - Sequence: put the steps of a process in order.
   - Word Bank Recall: fill in the missing term from a word bank, so
     students recall rather than only recognize.
   - Read the Data: tap the point or region on a graph or table that
     answers the question.
   - Claim and Evidence: pick a claim, then the evidence that supports it.
   - Lab Lock: an escape-room puzzle at the end of each world. Each solved
     clue gives one digit of the code that unlocks the scout pod, and the
     clues link ideas from across the world. End with a short debrief.
2. Feedback: after every answer, right or wrong, explain WHY in one or two
   sentences. After a wrong try, give a hint first, then let the student
   try again.
3. Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving (mixing topics):
   - Missed items return at the start of later levels and in later sessions.
   - From the second level of each world on, every level has a mixed-review
     gate with questions from earlier levels' topics. Missed items come
     first, then the ones seen longest ago.
   - Each world's boss mixes its own topic with earlier topics.
   - From World 2 on, each world also has review gates with items from
     earlier worlds, and the final Keystone Challenge mixes all six worlds.
   - Mixing runs throughout the game, not in a separate optional mode.
   - Look-alike pairs: questions on easily confused ideas (for example,
     diffusion and osmosis) are tagged as pairs, and review gates place
     the two side by side so students must tell them apart.
   - Planned for after World 3: a Keystone Review mode that mixes
     everything a student has unlocked, weighted toward weak topics.
4. Adaptive difficulty: tag every item level 1 (recall), 2 (apply), or 3
   (analyze data or a model). Step a student up after repeated first-try
   success on a Keystone code and down, with a hint, after misses.
5. Accuracy over speed: points come mainly from first-try accuracy. Any
   timer is optional and off by default, because time pressure causes
   stress and cuts down thinking.
6. Story: keep the alien field-report story throughout. Each level ends
   with a one-sentence field report the student chooses or completes.
7. Teamwork plus friendly competition: individual high scores, plus a
   class or team goal everyone contributes to (for example, powering the
   class mothership), and a Pair Mode where partners take turns and must
   say their reasoning aloud before choosing.
8. Built for repeated short sessions alongside teaching: levels are 2 to 4
   minutes, worlds unlock in course order, and a teacher guide suggests
   10 to 15 minute warm-ups across the unit.

## OVERARCHING GOAL (from the teacher; this outranks every other rule)
The game must help students EXPLORE, DISCOVER, and LEARN vocabulary and key concepts while they play. It must not be a review quiz disguised as a game. A beginner, even an 8-year-old, should be able to learn the basics just by playing. Quizzes stop the flow, and being stuck makes players quit, so:
- Teach before you test: every challenge comes after the player has discovered what it needs, by scanning specimens in the world (press E). Each scan gives a short note in kid-friendly words, adds one word to the Field Guide, and does not stop the game.
- Nobody gets stuck: after two wrong tries, the right choice glows gold.
- Wrong choices cost a few points only. Only enemies and hazards cost energy.
- Prefer doing over answering: build, sort, choose a path, operate a body system. Use as few written question stations as possible.
